The meeting ends, everyone leaves, and someone has to sit down and write the minutes. That job usually lands on the secretary or on whoever drew the short straw. They stare at half-legible notes for two hours, trying to reconstruct who said what and what was actually agreed. The minutes go out late, important decisions are missing, and nobody is sure who owns which action item.

This guide covers how to produce meeting minutes automatically: record the meeting, transcribe it, and let AI draft the summary. It is written for managers, secretaries, HR professionals, and anyone who runs meetings in a company, a government office, or a school.

## What meeting minutes are and what they should contain

Meeting minutes are the official record of what was discussed and decided. Good minutes include:

- the date, time, location, and list of attendees;
- the agenda, meaning which topics were covered;
- a short summary of the discussion on each topic;
- the decisions that were made;
- action items: who will do what, and by when.

The last two items matter most. They turn a meeting from talk into work. They are also where manual minutes tend to fail, because nobody can capture every sentence while also taking part in the conversation.

## Three problems with writing minutes by hand

**1. Divided attention.** The note-taker has to listen and write at the same time, and it is hard to do both well. A key sentence slips past while you are finishing the previous one.

**2. Unclear attribution.** In a meeting with five or ten people, voices blur together. Later someone asks, "Who said that?" and no one can answer.

**3. Time.** Writing up a one-hour meeting often takes another hour or two. By then the minutes are late and the deadlines on the action items have already started running.

## How automatic meeting minutes work

Modern AI meeting notes follow three steps: record, transcribe, summarize.

1. **Record.** A phone on the table captures the audio for the whole meeting.
2. **Transcribe.** Software converts speech to text. A good system also separates speakers (speaker diarization), so the transcript shows "Speaker 1", "Speaker 2", and so on, which you can rename to real names.
3. **Summarize.** AI reads the transcript and pulls out topics, decisions, and action items, including who is responsible for each.

The note-taker stops being a stenographer and becomes a participant: listening, asking questions, and reviewing the finished draft afterwards.

## Practical tips for better results

- **Place the phone well.** Put it in the middle of the table, away from papers and clutter. Anything that touches the phone makes noise.
- **One speaker at a time.** This helps the software, and it makes for a better meeting anyway.
- **Do quick introductions.** If everyone says their name at the start, labeling speakers afterwards is much easier.
- **Read the agenda aloud.** The AI separates topics more reliably when they are stated out loud.
- **Repeat the action items at the end.** Something like: "To sum up: Aziz sends the report tomorrow, Dilnoza approves the contract on Friday." It is a clear signal for the people in the room and for the AI.
- **Always review the output.** AI saves time, but it does not remove responsibility. Before you sign off on official minutes, check names, numbers, and deadlines yourself. Noisy rooms, people talking over each other, and strong accents can all reduce transcription accuracy.

## A note on privacy

Tell participants before you record and get their consent. It is good practice, and in many places it is a legal requirement. For meetings involving confidential information, decide in advance who will have access to the recording and the notes.
